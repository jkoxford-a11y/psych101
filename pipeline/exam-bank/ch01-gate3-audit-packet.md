## Grounding passages

Short excerpts from `source/chapters/ch01-history-approaches.md` (and a couple of Ch. 2 references for the three Integration items), provided so you can judge correctness without needing the textbook itself.

**Definition of psychology (LO1 — Items 1.1–1.2):** "Psychology is the scientific study of behavior and mental processes... 'Behavior' means anything an organism does that is observable and measurable... 'Mental processes' means the internal events that drive behavior... inferred from behavior, self-report, physiology, and neural activity... you flinch at a sudden loud sound before you consciously register what made it. The flinch is the behavior... The split-second appraisal that decided 'that sound is worth reacting to' is the mental process." The field spans basic research, applied science, clinical/counseling work, and more — not only clinical practice.

**Historical schools (LO2 — Items 2.1–2.6):** Wundt founded an experimental laboratory and worked within a broader framework often called voluntarism; Titchener developed structuralism more specifically — both relied on trained introspection, and "introspective reports were unreliable across observers." Functionalism (James) asked "what is the mind for?" — connecting mind to adaptation and function. Behaviorism (Watson, Skinner) explained behavior through environment, action, and consequence; "Watson's methodological behaviorism restricted psychology to publicly observable behavior. Skinner's radical behaviorism treated private events — thinking, feeling, imagining — as behavior too, while insisting that naming an inner event does not explain it. Explanation still had to be grounded in environmental histories and functional relations." In 1957 Skinner argued language could be explained through learned verbal behavior; in 1959 Chomsky argued this didn't explain how children rapidly produce and understand novel sentences — "Chomsky's critique became one important pressure toward cognitive explanation; it was not a single handoff that ended behaviorism, and it did not show that reinforcement contributes nothing to language." Psychoanalysis (Freud) drew attention to unconscious motivation but "claims were structured so that no outcome could disconfirm them — weak falsifiability (Popper, 1959)." Humanistic psychology (Maslow, Rogers) arose as "a reaction against behaviorism and psychoanalysis" — both traditions were seen as "missing something: human agency, growth, and the experience of being a person."

**Modern perspectives (LO3 — Items 3.1–3.5):** Seven perspectives (biological, behavioral, cognitive, psychodynamic, humanistic, sociocultural, evolutionary) each frame a different question. "The biological perspective asks how a mechanism works, the evolutionary perspective asks what it was built to do... Psychologists call these a proximate explanation and an ultimate explanation." "A perspective can make a question visible without making every answer from that perspective equally well supported. Evidence still decides among competing claims." Phone-checking example: behavioral, cognitive, sociocultural, and evolutionary answers are all given, and "none of these four answers has to contradict the others... each is answering a different question about the same five seconds of behavior." Depression example: biological, behavioral, cognitive, and sociocultural accounts are "evidence-informed levels of analysis," while historical psychodynamic accounts "are less strongly supported as a general causal account of depression than the risk and maintenance processes above."

**Biopsychosocial model (LO4 — Items 4.1–4.3):** "Biological, psychological, and social/environmental factors can interact to produce behavior and mental states. The model is an organizing framework for what to consider, not itself a causal explanation — a complete account still has to specify the actual mechanisms and how they interact in a given case."

**Evolutionary perspective (LO5 — Items 5.1–5.2):** "The evolutionary perspective's defining question is why a mechanism exists — its adaptive function — not just what differences are present across species." Risk: "generating untestable 'just-so' stories about why a trait evolved (Gould & Lewontin, 1979)." "Evolutionary hypotheses are tested by deriving predictions that can be examined using comparative, developmental, cross-cultural, genetic, and ecological evidence."

**Hindsight bias, confirmation bias, replication, self-correction (LO6 — Items 6.1–6.5):** "Hindsight bias is the tendency, after learning an outcome, to believe you knew it all along." "Confirmation bias: the tendency to seek out, interpret, and remember information that confirms existing beliefs, and to discount information that challenges them." The replication crisis: "beginning around 2011, researchers began systematically attempting to replicate published findings and discovered that a substantial number did not hold up... The replication crisis is not a reason to dismiss psychology. It revealed serious weaknesses in research incentives, statistical practices, publication systems, and evidential standards... Biased inference does not require deliberate fraud." "Self-correction is possible, not automatic — it took deliberate reform, not a guarantee built into doing science."

**AI Connection (Items AI.1–AI.2):** "A language model can generate a convincing answer. It can even produce the sentence 'I am conscious.' That fluent self-report is observable output, not evidence of subjective consciousness... what remains incomplete is the causal explanation for why those operations produced a particular response." "In 1950, Alan Turing proposed evaluating machine intelligence through observable performance... That was not the same dispute Skinner and Chomsky were having, but the questions intersect. Both force us to ask what successful language behavior can tell us about the process that produced it."

**Integration (Items INT.1–INT.3):** Ch. 1: confirmation bias content (above). Ch. 2 (referenced): "The double-blind procedure... blocks participants from performing what they are 'supposed' to feel, and researchers from unconsciously treating the groups differently" — described in Ch. 2 as "a direct guard against the confirmation bias from Chapter 1." Ch. 1: replication-crisis content (above). Ch. 2 (referenced): "Because every statistical decision rule permits some errors, a significant result still requires replication."
