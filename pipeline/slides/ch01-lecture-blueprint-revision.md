**Against:** the 40-slide draft blueprint (chat, 2026-07-29)

**Grounded in:** `source/chapters/ch01-history-approaches.md`, `pipeline/ch01-spine-seeds.md` (decisions returned 2026-07-27), `pipeline/evidence-commitments.md` → Chapter 1

Four things change: the slide count closes against the clock, Section 2 stops doing Section 4's job, generator field 9 (core/optional) gets applied to slides rather than only to activities, and three slides are re-pointed at what the chapter now actually says.

## 3. Section 2 stops doing Section 4's job

This is the structural change, and it is worth more than the cuts.

Slides 19–22 in the draft are epistemology slides wearing history costumes. They teach inference-to-mechanism and falsifiability — Section 4's subject — from inside the history section, which both bloats Section 2 (thirteen slides) and means the chapter's climax arrives at slide 31 of 40, i.e. the part that gets cut when the room runs slow.

**Move the merged 19+20 (black-box inference) into Section 4, immediately before slide 36 (anti-bias machinery).** It becomes the bridge from "intuition fails" to "here is the machinery" — students propose converging evidence, *then* see the institutional version of the same move. In Section 2 it was a digression; in Section 4 it is the argument.

Section 2 then runs: divider → question map → Wundt/James (merged) → behaviorism → Freud/humanistic → cognitive return → question sort → synthesis table. Eight slides, all of them history.

**One sequencing bug in the question sort (draft slide 15).** It asks students to classify four questions, two of which — "what consequence keeps avoidance going" (behaviorism, slide 16) and "what neural systems produce the response" (biological, slide 25) — have not been taught yet at slide 15. Move it to the end of Section 2 where it retrieves the whole section instead of pre-testing two-thirds of it.

## 4. Rewritten slides

### s7 — The camera test *(was 6 items × 4 categories)*

Four categories collapse to three. "Physiological evidence" and "self-report evidence" are not a third and fourth *kind of thing* — they are both evidence used to license an inference, which is the distinction the slide is actually teaching.

> **Could a camera record it?**

Reveal one at a time:

* A student closes a laptop.
* A student's heart rate increases.
* A student says, "I am not nervous."
* A student feels embarrassed.

Students sort into:

* **observable behavior**
* **inferred mental process**
* **evidence used to make the inference**

The third and fourth items are the load-bearing ones: a self-report is *observable behavior* that serves as *evidence* about a mental process, and it can be false. That is the whole lesson, and six items dilutes it.

**Bottom claim** (absorbing the cut slide 8):
> One behavior can be consistent with more than one internal process.

**Misconception repaired:** "internal" does not mean scientifically inaccessible.

---

### s20 — Black-box inference *(absorbs 19; now core; now in Section 4)*

Three hidden systems produce the same short answer:

* a person
* a scripted lookup system
* a language model

**Figure caption:** three pathways, one output.

> **Performance tells us what a system can do. It does not, by itself, tell us how.**
>
> What evidence would distinguish the mechanisms?

**Activity — 4 min, core.** Pairs propose two pieces of converging evidence. Categories, revealed only after they have tried: novel transfer, learning trajectory, error patterns, intervention, internal measurement, response to changed conditions.

**Why core:** the deck lets students *experience* "intuition fails" twice (Milgram, Wason) but only *asserts* "evidence assigns weight" — the lens switch has them generate, and slide 28 has the instructor deliver the calibration. This is the one slide where a student's own proposal gets tested and can fall short. Without it the second half of the governing argument is something students are told.

**Boundary:** this is not an AI lecture. If the room takes it there, the answer is that the same question was asked about Skinner and Chomsky twenty slides ago.

---

### s21 — Mechanism is not function *(absorbs 22; MOVES to Section 3)*

**This slide is in the wrong section as of 2026-07-27.** `pipeline/ch01-spine-seeds.md` installed proximate/ultimate as named vocabulary in **Section 3** (Insertion A at the perspectives paragraph, Insertion B after the phone-checking bullets). The draft blueprint teaches the distinction in Section 2, where the chapter no longer names it. Move the slide to Section 3, directly after the seven-question map, and let it carry the vocabulary the chapter now uses.

**Biological question**
> What physical mechanism produces this?

**Evolutionary question**
> Why might a mechanism like this exist at all?

Example: attraction to sweet foods. Mechanism candidates — taste receptors, hypothalamic and reward systems. Function hypothesis — ancestral foods with sweetness often supplied energy.

> Psychologists call these **proximate** and **ultimate** explanations.

Then (absorbing 22):
> **What evidence could make the function claim fail?**
> comparative · developmental · cross-cultural · genetic · ecological

**Bottom claim:**
> Explaining how a trait works does not explain why it exists. And a plausible story about why is not yet evidence.

**Instructor watch** (from the spine seed, verbatim intent): do not let this collapse into "biological = wrong, evolutionary = right." A mechanism presented alone looks arbitrary; an ultimate account alone makes the mechanism look dispensable. The chapter's Gould & Lewontin (1979) just-so-stories caveat in the Section 2 table stays as-is and is not restated here.

**Do not** import ecological dominance–social competition. That belongs to Ch. 11's deferred work.

Seven rows × three columns at a 30-point floor does not fit, revealed or not. Biology and Evolution move out — they are now taught in Section 3 with the proximate/ultimate pair, so repeating them here duplicates a slide that is four minutes away.

| Question | What it revealed | What it left out |
|---|---|---|
| Experience | Conscious contents | Reliable measurement |
| Function | Adaptation and purpose | Unified method |
| Behavior | Learning relations | Generative cognition |
| Unconscious | Processes outside awareness | Falsifiability |
| Cognition | Internal information processing | Embodiment and context |

Reveal two rows at a time. The third column is the reason this slide exists; if the room is behind, reveal only the third column against row labels already shown.

> The field's perspectives are scar tissue from productive arguments.

### s32 — Return to the prediction *(citation fix)*

`evidence-commitments.md` Chapter 1 requires: *"Attribute the observed obedience result to Milgram (1963) and source the psychiatrists' forecast separately."* The draft slide cites neither. Two citations, not one — and they are different sources, which is itself the teaching point.

* Panel of 39 psychiatrists predicted about **1 in 1,000** would go to the maximum *(Milgram, 1974)*
* In the condition Milgram first published: **26 of 40** — about two in three *(Milgram, 1963)*

> How obvious does the result feel now?
> Now look at what you actually predicted.

**Add to instructor notes — this is the draft's live vulnerability.** The deck stakes its climactic evidence demonstration on a study whose own evidentiary record is contested (variable prodding across runs, debriefing practices, condition-to-condition variability). A student who has read about this can undercut a lecture whose thesis is *evidence beats confident intuition*, and the draft gives the instructor nothing to say.

The fix is an upgrade, not a retreat — two lines, spoken:

> This study has its own problems, and you will meet them in Chapter 11. That is not a reason to throw out the result. It is the reason slide 36 exists.

That converts the objection into the section's argument. The prediction failure is what the slide needs, and the prediction failure is not what is contested.

**Forward pointer:** the chapter's Connections table routes this study to Ch. 11, not Ch. 2. Slide 40 currently points only to Ch. 2.

### s34 — Discover my rule *(boundary added)*

Keep as written. One addition to the instructor notes, in the same spirit as the Milgram note:

The standard "confirmation bias" reading of 2-4-6 is itself contested — positive testing is often a rational search strategy, not a defect. Debrief on **what a test risks**, not on whether students "have" a bias. The draft's existing boundary line ("a classroom demonstration of a reasoning tendency, not a measurement of an individual student's rationality") already points here; make it explicit in the notes so it survives into the deck.

> A good test risks telling you that you are wrong.

---

### s38 — Close *(absorbs 39)*

> **Is psychology common sense dressed up in fancy language?**

Two sentences, written, no notes:

1. Concede what makes the misconception attractive.
2. Explain why it fails.

Then reveal the skeleton as the answer key, not as the task:

> Psychology studies **behavior** and **mental processes** using **methods that can show us we are wrong**.
> Perspectives generate **candidates**; evidence assigns **weight**.

Prose first, skeleton second. The draft's order — fill blanks, then answer the opener — spends the last three minutes of class on the easier task.

## 5. Revised route

**Core, 50 minutes — 28 slides**

Opening 3 · Section 1 five · Section 2 eight · Section 3 seven · Section 4 five

Activities: Milgram predict (5–6) · camera test (3) · lens switch (6–7) · black-box inference (4) · Wason (6–7) · close (3) = **27–30 min**

That is over budget by any honest reading, which is the real finding: **six activities do not fit in 50 minutes.** Pick five. The droppable one is the camera test — it is the only activity whose work slide 9 partly redoes. Dropping it lands at 24–27 minutes of activity and ~26 minutes of talk across 27 slides.

**Expanded, 75 minutes — 34 slides**

Restore: the flinch, Wundt/Titchener as separate slides, levels-by-timescale, biopsychosocial mechanism building, the two biology/evolution synthesis rows, the historical question sort as a standalone.

## 6. One generator change

Field 9 (*core or optional status*) exists in the draft's generator spec but no slide in the draft uses it — only activities are tiered. Every slide record should carry it, and the 50-minute route should be generated by filtering on it rather than by the instructor deciding mid-lecture what to skip. A slide cut in the room is a slide cut badly.

Field 12 (*content intentionally omitted from the slide*) is the best idea in the spec and should be mandatory. Field 8 (*joke/story/demo slot*) should be nullable — required humor produces filler.
