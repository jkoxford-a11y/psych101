# Mature 4:3 Lecture Deck Reference Patterns

**Purpose:** compact, repo-friendly reference for agents building Psych101 lecture decks. The external visual reference is `ch10_lifespan_development_4x3_draft.pptx`; this file preserves representative teaching moves and note structure without committing generated PPTX binaries.

Use with `pipeline/deck-spec.md`. These are exemplars, not templates to copy mechanically.

---

## 1. Prediction → misconception repair

### PREDICTION

**Claim/task:** Predict before you know.

**Student prompt:** Which statement best captures development? “Children are mostly incomplete adults.” Commit first. Then explain your reason to a neighbor.

**Why it works:** the next slide has something to repair because students created a prior commitment.

### CORE CLAIM

**Claim:** Development does not simply add facts. It rebuilds how a person represents and solves a problem.

**Structure:** conservation case → child answer → mechanism-level interpretation.

**Boundary:** use conservation as a durable teaching case, not a full endorsement of rigid Piagetian stages.

Source: Chapter 10 mature deck.

## 2. Roadmap compression

### ROADMAP

**Title:** Today’s four questions.

1. How do we study change over time?
2. How do biology and experience build each other?
3. How do minds develop with other people?
4. What reorganizes after childhood?

**Why it works:** seven formal objectives become four retention questions. The slide is a problem map, not an assessment rubric.

Source: Chapter 10 mature deck.

## 3. Mechanism instead of vocabulary list

### MECHANISM

**Claim:** Humans follow a slow, expensive strategy.

**Teaching structure:** finite-energy constraint → fast/slow life-history continuum → human cluster of large brain, long childhood, late reproduction, heavy care.

**Boundary:** avoid ladder language; this is a tradeoff continuum, not a ranking of organisms.

Source: Chapter 10 mature deck.

---

## 4. System map instead of single cause

### SYSTEM MAP

**Claim:** Long childhood requires a social solution.

**Structure:** extended human development at center, surrounded by expensive brain, shared care, social learning, ecology, and secondary altriciality.

**Bottom line:** coevolved contributors, not one first cause.

Source: Chapter 10 mature deck.

## 5. Methods taught as inference

### COMPARISON

**Claim:** Two designs ask different change questions. Age difference is not automatically aging.

**Cross-sectional:** different people, one testing year; signature weakness = cohort effects.

**Longitudinal:** same people retested; signature weaknesses = attrition and practice effects.

**Diagnostic question:** Are we comparing ages, cohorts, or within-person change?

Source: Chapter 10 mature deck.

---

## 6. Core tool

### CORE TOOL

**Claim:** Memory has three places to fail. “I forgot” is not a diagnosis.

- Encoding — never built well enough.
- Storage — not stabilized or maintained.
- Retrieval — available but not accessible now.

**Diagnostic question:** Where did the failure happen?

**Why it works:** three textbook terms become a reusable inference tool.

Source: Chapter 8 mature deck.

---

## 7. Evidence as a method story

### CLASSIC EVIDENCE

**Claim:** Sperling separated what was available from what could be reported.

**Structure:** 50 ms letter grid → whole-report performance → partial-report performance → inference.

**Boundary:** brief availability is not a permanent photographic trace.

Source: Chapter 8 mature deck.

---

## 8. Do Not Confuse

### DO NOT CONFUSE

**Claim:** System 1 is not “bad”; System 2 is not “good.” The distinction is processing mode, not moral quality.

**Structure:** parallel cards + bottom warning that slow reasoning can carefully apply the wrong rule.

**Boundary:** the labels summarize modes; they are not literal brain structures or a complete theory of judgment.

Source: Chapter 9 mature deck.

---

## 9. Prediction with a failure mode

### PREDICTION

**Prompt:** More English words beginning with K, or with K as the third letter?

Students choose and write one sentence explaining how they judged.

**Debrief:** ease of retrieval is not the same as frequency.

**Why it works:** availability is experienced before it is named.

Source: Chapter 9 mature deck.

---

## 10. Synthesis and close

### SYNTHESIS

A mature synthesis reconstructs the chapter’s argument rather than listing key terms.

Example Chapter 10 claim:

> Development is slow construction under biological, social, and historical constraint.

The slide asks students to supply examples for biology, experience, relationships, and context.

### RETRIEVAL CLOSE

Return to the opening misconception. Require a short generated answer, often:

1. explain why the misconception is attractive;
2. explain why it fails using one chapter mechanism or distinction.

Source: Chapters 8–10 mature decks.

## 11. Required mature note block

```text
Intent: [why the slide exists]
Instructor move: [what to explain/ask/emphasize]
Activity procedure: [exact steps or None]
Reveal intention: [sequence or Static]
Evidence boundary: [needed boundary or None needed]
Source anchor: [chapter section/figure/study]
Transition: [why the next slide follows]
Optional elaboration: [story/joke/example or None]
Status: [core/optional/backup]
Intentionally omitted: [what was deliberately kept off-screen]
```

Generic notes such as “Explain this slide” do not meet the standard.
