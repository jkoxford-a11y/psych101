## The seed

**Spine claim:** Biological and evolutionary aren't two more items in a seven-row list — they're two different *kinds* of question about the same mechanism: one asks how it works right now, the other asks what it was built to do. Naming that distinction is free, because the chapter already asks both questions; it just never says they're a pair.

**Lead with:** what's already there. Section 3's perspectives table already has a Biological row ("What physical mechanisms are involved?") sitting next to an Evolutionary row ("What adaptive problem might this mechanism have helped solve?"). The "checking your phone" walkthrough right after it already gives the evolutionary bullet an ultimate answer in every way but name: *"asks why a brain would be built to find social information so hard to ignore in the first place."* The vocabulary is the only thing missing.

**Arc:** setup (seven perspectives, seven questions, presented as parallel) → tension (Biological and Evolutionary look like they're competing for the same slot — "which one really explains phone-checking?") → payoff (**they were never competing — one names the mechanism, one names why a mechanism like that exists at all, and you need both or the mechanism looks arbitrary**).

**Payoff line seed (two short insertions, not one block):**

*Insertion A — Section 3, in the "A few of these are worth one more sentence" paragraph (line 162), immediately after the existing sentence about the biological perspective getting its own chapter:*

> Biological and evolutionary are not two competing answers to the same question — the biological perspective asks how a mechanism works, the evolutionary perspective asks what it was built to do, and a psychologist usually wants both. Psychologists call these **proximate** and **ultimate** explanations; you'll see the pair again in Chapters 7, 8, 10, 11, and 13.

*Insertion B — Section 3, "One Behavior, Several Questions" (line 171), directly after the existing evolutionary bullet about phone-checking:*

> That last question is a different kind from the other three. It isn't asking what's driving you to check your phone right now — it's asking why a brain built to do that would exist in the first place.

**Prune candidate:** the "A few of these are worth one more sentence" paragraph (line 162) already runs long — the clause on applied behavior analysis's "careful ethical discussion about goals, consent, and quality of life" is a tangent this section doesn't otherwise develop. ~20 words available if Insertion A needs room. Net addition even without taking this prune is ~75 words against a 742-word section — not a real budget problem, but naming it per protocol.

**Watch:**
- Do not let this collapse into "biological = wrong, evolutionary = right." Trunk §7 names this as the most common framing failure in the base drafts — a mechanism presented alone looks arbitrary or defective, but presenting only the ultimate side makes the proximate mechanism look dispensable. Both insertions above name both directions.
- Do not import ED–SC (ecological dominance–social competition) here. That's a separate, further claim about *why* human ultimate explanations run through social competition specifically — it belongs where Ch. 11's deferred work picks it up, not in this general, definitional introduction of the proximate/ultimate pair.
- Do not overstate the evolutionary perspective's evidentiary standing beyond what the chapter already says. The existing "just-so stories" caveat (Gould & Lewontin, 1979) in the Section 2 table stays as-is; this seed doesn't touch it.
