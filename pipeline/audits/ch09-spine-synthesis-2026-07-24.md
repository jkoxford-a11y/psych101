## 2. The proposed spine (one sentence, then the mechanism)

**Cognition is prediction under a hard constraint.** A limited system cannot store or recompute everything, so it compresses experience into concepts and uses them to predict what it has not directly seen. The quality of your predictions is set by the quality of your concepts, and concepts improve with more — and better-organized — experience.

That single idea carries all four sections. It is also already latent in the current chapter (the compression metaphor, assimilation/accommodation, prototype/exemplar); the instructor wants it promoted from recurring motif to backbone.

## 3. Section-by-section, with the evidence

### Section 1 — Concepts (the compression). Rock-solid.
Concepts are the compression of experience; prototype vs. exemplar, basic-level categories, and novice vs. expert are all descriptions of how richly experience has been organized.

**Empirical anchor: Chase & Simon (1973), "Perception in Chess."** Masters reconstruct real board positions after a 5-second glance; novices cannot — *but the master's advantage collapses on random boards.* Same ordinary working-memory span; the difference is bigger, meaningful *chunks*. This is the cleanest possible demonstration of the spine: same limited machine, better concepts, better perception and prediction — and it proves the gain comes from *structured concepts*, not raw memory. (Also lands the instructor's own "Chess master opener" voice tag for this chapter, and pairs with the SF digit-span 7→79 chunking story he already uses.)

**Update mechanism: Piaget.** Assimilation applies an existing concept; accommodation rebuilds it when a prediction fails. This is already in the chapter (para 66) and just needs to be named as *how the concept improves*.

Verdict: keep and foreground. This is the instructor's "favorite part" (C2) and the evidence is unambiguous.

### Section 2 — Heuristics & biases (what compression costs). Keep the spine, calibrate the claim.
A heuristic is a concept-based prediction that is usually right and wrong in a characteristic way. This folds cleanly. The care is in C22–C24, the "biases are novice errors / Dunning-Kruger" claim. The literature says: **half right, and the other half matters.**

- *Supported:* richer domain concepts improve specific judgments. Experts *do* use base rates in realistic, sequential decisions where lab novices neglect them (Experts use base rates in real-world sequential decisions, PMC9038831). So a novice with few instances genuinely does "fill in" more from the prototype — the pedagogical point survives.
- *Not supported:* expertise is **not** a bias vaccine. Experts remain vulnerable, and can be *more* biased, through the very schema/heuristic machinery that makes them expert (forensic-bias and expert-decision reviews; JAAPL 53:2). The conjunction fallacy persists in statistically trained people.
- *Dunning–Kruger is contested.* Since Gignac & Zajenkowski (2020) there is a live debate that the effect is substantially a statistical artifact (better-than-average + regression to the mean), though a residual metacognitive account survives. It is too shaky to be load-bearing.

**Recommended framing:** "Better concepts sharpen your predictions *in your domain* — a novice fills the gaps from a thin prototype, an expert from a dense one." Use Dunning–Kruger as a light, explicitly-caveated illustration, not a pillar. This keeps the instructor's insight *and* stays honest.

### Section 3 — Language (how concepts get shared). Fold, do not cut. This is the strongest fold.
Language is the compression *protocol* that lets one mind load concepts into another — the reason a semester can install concepts the field took a century to build. This is exactly the instructor's own C25 instinct ("our concepts are shaped by semantics, thus language").

The evidence gives a *real*, not merely metaphorical, bridge:
- **Huth et al. (2016) semantic maps:** concepts are represented as distributed cortical maps built from *statistical word co-occurrence*, consistent across people. Concepts have real, learned neural structure shaped by linguistic experience.
- **Statistical learning (Saffran 1996) + social gating (Kuhl 2003):** language, like expertise, is structure extracted from experience — same *kind* of process as concept-building.

Reframe linguistic relativity as: shared linguistic categories shape which distinctions you predict/notice easily (the Russian-blues and spatial-frame results already in the chapter fit this without change).

Verdict: fold as "how concepts are transmitted and shared." Cutting it would also break the APA Cognition-pillar structure and the 13-chapter architecture.

### Section 4 — Intelligence (measuring concept quality and concept-building capacity). Fold; re-weight.
- **Crystallized intelligence = accumulated concept-richness.** Folds perfectly.
- **Fluid intelligence = the rule-finding you do when you do *not* yet have the concept** — the engine that builds new concepts. (The existing Finding-the-Rule lab already teaches exactly this.) The Gf→new learning→Gc loop *is* the spine's growth curve.
- ***g*, IQ, Flynn Effect** = attempts to measure the whole and evidence that concept-building responds to environment (schooling, nutrition, exposure to abstraction) — which is the spine's optimistic corollary.
- **Gardner / Sternberg** = arguments about whether the measured concept-set is broad enough. They earn a place as *critique of what tests omit*, not as co-equal theories. Trim multiple intelligences to a short, honest critique box (its psychometric support is weak; Waterhouse 2006 already cited).

Verdict: fold, foreground Gf/Gc-as-concept-machinery, demote (don't delete) Gardner/Sternberg.

## 4. The motivational payload (answers C0's "value of college")
The through-line from opener to summary: the value of college is not the facts — those you can look up, and an AI will generate them fluently. The value is building concepts good enough to *predict, evaluate, and use* facts you were never handed. Better concepts → better predictions → better work, including better work *with* AI, because a dense concept is what lets you catch the fluent answer that is wrong. (The existing "Fluency Is Not Accuracy" AI box already sets this up and should be promoted toward the spine.)

Education evidence backs the frame: meaningful learning — connecting new material to existing structure — transfers where rote memorization does not (APA "Practice for knowledge acquisition"; rote-vs-meaningful literature). This is the empirical warrant for "one good concept is worth a hundred memorized facts," which is already the chapter's "Where This Fits" hook.

## 5. Where I push back — what stays metaphor, honestly (loyal to the argument)
1. **No single shared neural mechanism** unifying Piaget, Chomsky's LAD, and semantic maps (C25). The evidence supports a **family resemblance** — concepts, language, and skilled cognition are all *structure extracted from experience to support prediction* — and that is a strong, defensible organizing frame. It does **not** support "the same neural machine." Keep the chapter's existing hedge, but make the *organizing* claim central and bold: same **kind** of thing, not same mechanism.
2. **Predictive processing** (Helmholtz → Clark/Friston) is the deep theory under "cognition is prediction." It is a leading but still-debated framework. Use it as a powerful lens, introduced honestly as a lens, not as settled fact. It lets the spine rest on real theory rather than only a metaphor.
3. **Dunning–Kruger** — light touch, explicit caveat (see §3.2).
4. **Compression** stays a *learning metaphor*, as the chapter already flags. The spine strengthens it by giving it a mechanism (prediction) rather than replacing the hedge.

Net: the instructor's thesis is genuinely good and largely evidence-backed; the only real overreach is the literal shared-mechanism claim, which we can honor as an organizing frame without asserting the neuroscience.

## 8. Integration of second-opinion review (2026-07-24)

A second reviewer pass was folded in. It does not overturn the spine; it sharpens it in the three places §2–§3 were softest. Accepted revisions:

**8.1 Spine articulation — add the operable loop.** Keep "cognition is prediction under constraint" as the *thesis* (it explains why concepts matter), but express it to students as a running refrain: **Build → Predict → Check → Revise.** The loop houses the whole chapter cleanly — concepts are Build, expertise is better Prediction, biases are what happens when a model is applied without the Check, and Piaget's accommodation is Revise. "Compression" stays as *what concepts accomplish*, not the thesis (consistent with the chapter's existing hedge).

**8.2 Scientific correction — exposure alone is not enough (supersedes the "more experience" phrasing in §2/§3.1).** Concept quality does not come from volume of exposure; experience must reveal **diagnostic structure**. The instructional levers that produce differentiated, transferable concepts are **contrasting cases** (Schwartz & Bransford, "A time for telling," 1998) and **interleaving** (Kornell & Bjork, 2008 — improves inductive category learning even though learners rate it less effective). This is the actionable core of the "value of college" payload and connects to the desirable-difficulties material in Ch. 8. This corrects both the instructor's "exposure to more information" framing and this note's earlier "more experience" phrasing.

**8.3 Structural hinge — the double edge of expertise.** Use the *same* chess-expertise example for both the concepts win and the bias cost: expert concepts reveal deep structure novices miss, but a familiar strong idea can capture attention and block a better solution — the **Einstellung effect demonstrated in chess experts** (Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet, 2008). This becomes the empirical bridge connecting Section 1 (concepts), problem solving (mental set / functional fixedness), and Section 2 (biases) with one running case, replacing the current disconnected treatment.

**8.4 Sharper section calls.**
- *System 1/2 (C20):* decline a neurotransmitter-per-system account (they are processing modes, not chemical switches). The defensible, cited claim is that stress can shift control from working-memory-supported model-based toward habitual responding — not a universal "System 1 conversion" (Otto et al., 2013; Schwabe & Wolf).
- *Prototype/exemplar:* do **not** claim novices use prototypes and experts graduate to exemplars (this corrects the instructor's tracked edit in packet paras 24–25). Both representations operate together depending on category and task; the reliable expertise claim is richer, more differentiated, domain-specific representations with better access to diagnostic features.
- *Language:* compress the Skinner-vs-Chomsky acquisition survey (least connected to the spine) and refocus on labels as portable, shareable conceptual distinctions that let communities inherit and refine structure. Labels can facilitate novel category learning without categories being wholly language-dependent (Lupyan, Rakison & McClelland, 2007).
- *Functional fixedness (C19):* convert to a predict → attempt/commit → reveal sequence so students experience what was fixed before the answer is given.
- *Dunning–Kruger:* convergent with §3.2 — measurement critiques are serious (McIntosh et al.); use lightly, never as the unifying mechanism.
- *LO comment C1 ("Both the first verbs"):* bold both operational verbs in LO1 ("Distinguish" and "explain"), consistent with objective style.

**8.5 Verification flag.** The new citations (Schwartz & Bransford 1998; Kornell & Bjork 2008; Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet 2008; Otto et al. 2013; Schwabe & Wolf; Lupyan, Rakison & McClelland 2007; McIntosh et al.) are real and appear accurately characterized, but must be verified against source before landing in the canonical chapter, per the project accuracy discipline.

**Net:** the governing argument is now Build → Predict → Check → Revise, resting on prediction-under-constraint, with concept *quality* driven by diagnostic structure (contrast + interleaving) rather than raw exposure, and the double edge of expertise as the hinge that unifies concepts, problem solving, and bias.
