What Did the System Throw Away?

A Cross-Chapter Lab on Lossy Compression

Plan on 15–20 minutes.

Your mind does not preserve every available detail equally. It builds usable representations: a stable percept, the meaning of an event, an expectation about a group, or a label for a recurring pattern.

That usually works. It also means that some information is lost.

In this lab, you will complete four short stations. At each one, ask:

  1. What was preserved?
  2. What was discarded?
  3. When did the discarded information become important?

What the station shows

Perception must settle ambiguous input into a usable interpretation. Context helps determine which interpretation wins. The percept preserves coherence while making the unresolved ambiguity less visible.

This does not mean expectations can make any input look like anything. Context has its strongest influence when incoming evidence supports more than one interpretation.

What the station shows

Memory often preserves meaning better than exact membership. The list strongly activated the idea of sleep, even though the word itself was absent. Semantic organization usually helps memory by connecting related information. It can also make an expected word feel familiar enough to be mistaken for one that was presented.

Selecting the absent word is not evidence of poor memory. Not selecting it does not disprove the phenomenon. The reliable finding is a group-level tendency in semantic-associate false-memory tasks.

What the station shows

A social prototype summarizes the examples you have encountered. That is useful when you must form expectations from incomplete information. But the prototype cannot automatically preserve the sampling process that produced those examples.

Vivid or repeatedly encountered members can shape an expectation about an entire group even when they are not representative. The problem is not categorization by itself. The problem is forgetting that the category was built from a particular—and possibly biased—sample.

Diagnosis: A Pattern Label Is Not a Causal Explanation

Morgan

Morgan’s difficulties began after a major loss. Withdrawal from friends and previously rewarding activities has become a major part of the continuing pattern.

Riley

Riley has experienced several episodes across multiple years. The current episode has no clear immediate precipitating event and includes severe disruption of sleep and daily rhythms.

Both fictional cases have received the same diagnostic label: major depressive disorder.

What does the shared diagnosis establish?

Diagnosis Reveal

The cases share a clinically recognized symptom pattern, but their causes, maintaining processes, and treatment needs may differ.

Diagnosis compresses a complex presentation into a shared pattern label. That label can support communication, research, eligibility decisions, and treatment planning. It does not contain the individual causal history inside it.

Two people can meet the same diagnostic criteria through different developmental paths and remain stuck for different reasons. The label organizes the pattern. A clinical formulation asks what produced and maintains it in this person.

    Phase 3 of 6 - Explicit Concept Explanation

    What the Four Stations Share

    What the completed ledger shows

    Across all four stations, the system kept enough structure to produce a useful answer:

    • perception produced a coherent percept;
    • memory retained semantic meaning;
    • social judgment formed an expectation;
    • diagnosis named a recurring symptom pattern.

    The errors appeared when the task later required information that had not been preserved.

    Lossy compression is a trade-off. Preserving everything would be slow, costly, and often unnecessary. Preserving what usually matters makes fast interpretation and action possible. The same economy creates predictable errors at the boundaries.

    Compression describes a shared functional trade-off, not one shared mechanism. Perception, memory, social judgment, and diagnosis do not operate through the same biological or computational process. They are being compared because each preserves some structure while losing other information.

    Do Not Confuse: shared trade-off ≠ shared mechanism

    These systems are not interchangeable. Context-sensitive perception, reconstructive memory, prototype formation, and clinical classification involve different evidence and mechanisms. Compression is the comparison being tested, not a claim that one process explains them all.

    Source

    In one introductory psychology course, students who chose to complete three optional practice quizzes scored higher on the unit exam than students who completed none. Because students chose whether to take the quizzes, the comparison cannot establish that the quizzes caused the score difference. The result also came from one course and did not compare every possible study method.

    Simulated AI summary

    ClaimCorrect classificationYour classification

    What the audit shows

    The summary preserved the broad association: students who completed the quizzes scored higher. It discarded self-selection, causal uncertainty, unspecified effect size, and the limited setting.

    Those details became important when the summary changed an association into a causal claim and generalized one classroom result to every course. Fluent compression preserved the story while weakening its evidential boundaries.

    Phase 6 of 6 - Completion Summary

    What You Practiced

    You practiced identifying what a representation preserves, what it loses, and when that loss becomes consequential. Compression is not the opposite of intelligent functioning. It is one of the conditions that makes intelligent functioning possible—and one reason intelligent systems make patterned mistakes.

    Instructor Note

    Assign this lab after students have completed Chapters 4, 8, 11, and 13, or use it as a cumulative final-unit activity. Have pairs compare which station most strongly tempted them toward an overconfident judgment.

    The key debrief is not “all mistakes are compression.” It is the narrower comparison: each system preserved structure that was usually useful, and the error appeared when discarded information became relevant.

    Do not focus on whether individual students selected the false-memory lure or made the biased group judgment. Use class patterns only as discussion material, never as evidence about an individual student.
