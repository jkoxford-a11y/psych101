Learning Lab - Chapter 9

Where Does It Belong?

Build, Test, and Revise a Concept

A definition tells you what a word means. A concept does more: it organizes examples, highlights useful similarities, marks important differences, and helps you predict what a new case will do.

In this 12–18 minute lab, you will build a map of a familiar category, test it against cases near the boundary, and then apply the same reasoning to a psychology concept that students often misclassify.

Teaching Activity, Accessibility, and Privacy

This is a teaching activity, not a test of intelligence, expertise, conceptual ability, memory, or bias. It stores only your lab responses and progress in sessionStorage for this browser-tab session. It stores no names, emails, student IDs, analytics, tracking data, or personal examples. Nothing is transmitted to a server.

Do not enter personal mental-health information or identifying information. You can complete every prompt using only the cases supplied here.

What You Will Practice

  1. Distinguish category membership from typicality.
  2. Distinguish prototypes from exemplars.
  3. Explain what atypical members and feature-sharing nonmembers reveal.
  4. Revise a concept after contrasting cases expose a weakness.
  5. Apply the same reasoning to the functional definition of reinforcement.

Text Event Log

    Phase 3: What the Map Shows

    Concept Reveal

    Concepts organize cases so that you can generalize beyond what you have already seen. That organization is not the same as memorizing a definition.

    A prototype captures what a typical member is like. An exemplar is a specific member retained in memory. Both can help you classify a new case.

    Typicality is graded. A robin may feel more bird-like than a penguin. Category membership asks a different question: does the case belong at all? A penguin can therefore be a clear member and an atypical example. A bat can resemble part of the prototype without becoming a member.

    Borderline and contrasting cases improve a concept because they reveal which similarities predict membership and which are merely familiar.

    A useful concept is not merely compact. It preserves the distinctions that support prediction and changes when the evidence no longer fits.

    Typicality judgments can vary across people and experience. Prototypes, exemplars, rules, and relational knowledge can work together; they are not mutually exclusive systems or successive stages every learner passes through.

    Do Not Confuse: a semantic map with a literal brain map

    The map on this page makes relationships among examples visible. It does not claim that every concept is stored as a two-dimensional picture. Concepts can draw on prototypes, remembered exemplars, rules, relations, context, and broader knowledge.

    Transfer Concept Reveal

    The everyday prototype of reinforcement is “something pleasant given as a reward.” That prototype handles the food-pellet case and fails on the others.

    The scientific concept is organized around a diagnostic relation: a consequence reinforces behavior when the behavior becomes more likely to occur again. Added versus removed determines positive versus negative. Increased versus decreased behavior determines reinforcement versus punishment.

    The better concept predicts cases the prototype misclassifies. Reinforcement is defined by the increase in future behavior, not by whether someone intended a reward or whether the consequence felt pleasant.

    Instructor Note

    Run this lab after the Chapter 9 concepts section or use it to retrieve Chapter 7's reinforcement distinction. The key discussion moment is the contrast between the penguin and the bat: membership and typicality are different judgments.

    In the transfer section, compare the $10 reward attempt with the seat-belt buzzer. Students often classify consequences by pleasantness or intention. The functional definition asks what happened to future behavior.

    Do not treat an individual student's map or misclassification as evidence of ability or bias. The activity is designed to produce revision, not to measure a stable trait.

    Evidence Note

    Chapter 9's prototype and typicality discussion, grounded in Rosch (1975), motivates the familiar-category comparison. The chapter also describes exemplars as specific remembered cases and treats prototype and exemplar accounts as compatible. The transfer section uses Chapter 7's functional definition: reinforcement is demonstrated when a consequence increases the future likelihood of behavior. These maps are instructional visualizations, not replications of one laboratory paradigm.
