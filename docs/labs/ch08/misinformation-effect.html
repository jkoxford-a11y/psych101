Did You Really See That?

You are going to read a brief description of a campus incident, answer some follow-up questions about it, complete a short interruption, and then take a memory test on specific details from the original scene.

This is a teaching demonstration, not a test of your memory ability or a controlled experiment on you. Its purpose is to let you experience the misinformation effect and then explain what happened.

The Incident

It was a Tuesday afternoon in the campus library. A student — let's call her Mia — was working at a table on the second floor, near the windows. Her textbooks were spread out in front of her, and she had set a brown laptop bag on the chair beside her.

Mia got up to find a book in the nearby stacks, leaving her table unattended. Shortly after, another person entered the study area — someone wearing a blue jacket, with short hair. They glanced around the room, walked directly to Mia's table, picked up the brown bag, and walked calmly toward the exit. A student at a nearby table glanced up briefly but said nothing.

Mia returned about three minutes later to find her bag gone.

Questions 2, 4, 6, and 7 each embedded a detail that was not in the original scene. Question 2 referred to "the person in the red jacket" (the jacket was blue). Question 4 mentioned "the person with long hair" (the hair was short). Question 6 described "the green bag" (the bag was brown). Question 7 said "when the person ran toward the exit" (the person walked calmly). Each of these is a presupposition — a detail slipped in as if already established — rather than the main point of the question.

What the activity was designed to show

This design adapts the presupposition technique from Loftus and Palmer's (1974) classic study, in which the word "smashed" (versus "contacted") caused participants to recall higher speed estimates and to falsely report broken glass that was never in the film. The mechanism is not deception: when you later reconstruct the event, the post-event information has been processed alongside the original fragments, and the reconstruction blends both without cleanly flagging their sources.

If your memory-test answers matched the implied details rather than the original scene, the effect worked as designed. If they matched the original scene, that is also consistent with the effect — it is probabilistic. Some people, some items, and some question phrasings produce more distortion than others. What Loftus and Palmer established is the reliability of the phenomenon at the group level, not that every individual will always be misled on every detail.

Do Not Confuse: Misinformation Effect with Gullibility or Lying

Experiencing the misinformation effect is not evidence of being inattentive, uncritical, or easily fooled. Loftus and Palmer's participants watched a film carefully. The effect arises because reconstructive memory blends all information encountered about an event without cleanly flagging each piece's origin. A person reporting a misinformed detail is not lying; they sincerely believe it is accurate. The underlying mechanism is source misattribution: the detail from the question is remembered correctly, but its source — the question itself — is confused with the source of the original encoding.

Instructor Note

Run before the reconstructive memory section to generate a prediction-and-surprise sequence, or afterward as an experiential check. Display class aggregate results — how many students chose each memory-test option — rather than focusing on individual scores. Questions 2, 4, 6, and 7 carry the misinformation; tracking which embedded details were most disruptive typically shows exit behavior (Question 7) and jacket color (Question 2) as strongest because those presuppositions are phrased most forcefully. Emphasize that the misinformation effect is a property of normal reconstructive memory, not a measure of intelligence or attentiveness.

Evidence Base

The design adapts the presupposition technique from Loftus and Palmer (1974). This version uses a text-based scenario rather than a film clip, which sacrifices the original study's film-fidelity while preserving the core misinformation delivery mechanism. The interpretation is qualified by the probabilistic nature of the effect and individual variability in susceptibility to leading questions.
